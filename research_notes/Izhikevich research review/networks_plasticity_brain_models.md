# Izhikevich's network-level work, c. 2003-2010: polychronization, dopamine-modulated STDP and large-scale brain simulation

Scope note: these notes cover the network, plasticity and large-scale simulation papers from Izhikevich's years at The Neurosciences Institute (NSI, San Diego), roughly 2003-2010. The 2003 "simple model", the 2004 "which model" paper and the 2007 *Dynamical Systems in Neuroscience* book are covered by another researcher and appear here only as context. Primary PDFs were downloaded from izhikevich.org and read directly. Quoted numbers come from those PDFs unless another source is cited.

## Q1. "Polychronization: Computation with Spikes" (Neural Computation 2006): definitions, model, capacity claims, links to synfire chains, follow-up work and critiques

### Takeaway
Izhikevich showed that a 1000-neuron random network with axonal delays of 1-20 ms and STDP self-organizes into thousands of "polychronous groups". These are anatomically defined chains whose spikes are time-locked but not synchronous, and the paper reported more groups than neurons. It presented this as "unprecedented memory capacity" and linked it to Edelman's neural Darwinism, binding, attention and consciousness. The most detailed later test, Pauli et al. (2018), reproduced the network and found the group counts very fragile: they depend on the integration time step, the STDP pairing scheme, the random seed and parameters of the detection algorithm. Pauli et al. concluded that the original "significantly overstates the number of groups." Other follow-ups (Guise, Knott and Benuskova; Martinez and Paugam-Moisy; Szatmary and Izhikevich; Eguchi, Stringer and colleagues) built on the concept rather than refuting it. I found no direct in vivo confirmation of delay-based polychronous groups.

### Cited Findings
**Publication facts and core claims**
- Citation: Izhikevich E.M. (2006), *Neural Computation* 18:245-282. The PDF carries a "© 2005 Massachusetts Institute of Technology" line and was "Communicated by Peter Thomas"; the author was at The Neurosciences Institute — [izhikevich.org PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/spnet.pdf)
- The abstract defines polychronization as "reproducible time-locked but not synchronous firing patterns with millisecond precision, as in synfire braids." It says that through "the interplay between the delays and STDP, the spiking neurons spontaneously self-organize into groups." It continues: "To our surprise, the number of coexisting polychronous groups far exceeds the number of neurons in the network, resulting in an unprecedented memory capacity of the system." The paper speculates on links to "TNGS, neural Darwinism", binding, the gamma rhythm, attention, and "consciousness as 'attention to memories'" — [izhikevich.org abstract page](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/spnet.htm)
- Etymology and distinctions: "poly means many and chronous means time or clock in Greek." The paper separates polychrony from asynchrony, which lacks reproducible time-locking, and from clustering, partial synchrony and "polysynchrony" (Stewart, Golubitsky and Pivato 2003) — [PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/spnet.pdf)
- Motivation from delays: citing Swadlow, the paper notes that cortical axonal conduction delays range from 0.1 ms to 44 ms but are reproducible with submillisecond precision for a given pair of neurons. It asks: "Why would the brain maintain different delays with such precision if spike timing were not important?" It argues that delay networks are infinite-dimensional and that this is "not a nuisance but an immense advantage" — [PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/spnet.pdf)
- Toy example (Fig. 2): neurons b, c and d project to a and e with different delays. Firing d, c, b at 0, 4 and 8 ms makes their spikes arrive at a together. The reverse order makes them arrive at e together. Synchronous firing excites neither — [PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/spnet.pdf)

**The minimal model**
- 1000 neurons: 80% excitatory and 20% inhibitory, connection probability 0.1, built from the 2003 simple neuron model. The model is simulated with 1 ms time resolution as a "finite-dimensional approximation." Conduction delays are random integers from 1 to 20 ms. Excitatory weights follow additive STDP (after Song, Miller and Abbott 2000) with a 10 mV weight cap, so "two presynaptic spikes are enough to fire a given postsynaptic cell." Neurons receive random "thalamic" input. The network settles for 24 h of model time, which "takes 6 hours on a 1 GHz PC" — [PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/spnet.pdf)
- Network dynamics: early delta-band (2-4 Hz) "sleeplike" oscillations give way to Poisson-like firing and then to gamma (30-100 Hz) rhythms as STDP reshapes the weights — [PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/spnet.pdf)
- Code: MATLAB (spnet.m) and C++ (spnet.cpp, poly_spnet.cpp) are provided, together with group-finding scripts (polygroup.m, polychron.m) — [abstract page](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/spnet.htm)

**How a group is defined and detected**
- A group is defined on "a purely anatomical basis; there is no firing involved yet." Given the connectivity and delays, a triplet of "anchor" neurons firing with a particular pattern, e.g. (125, 275, 490) at (0, 3, 7) ms, sets off a cascade through a subgraph. The group is "only an anatomical prediction of a possible stereotypical firing pattern." The 1 ms precision is "the consequence of our definition"; other spike patterns are treated as "noise" — [PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/spnet.pdf)
- A group counts as activated ("half-activated") when at least 50% of its excitatory neurons fire with the prescribed pattern within ±1 ms jitter. The paper stresses that this is "drastically different" from searching for synfire chains in in vivo data: "we know what the patterns are... Apparently such an approach is feasible only in models" — [PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/spnet.pdf)
- In its list of open problems, the author says the algorithm (which tries all triplets with all timings) is "extremely inefficient" because of combinatorial explosion, and that "we probably miss many groups that do not start with three neurons" — [PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/spnet.pdf)

**Quantitative results and capacity claim**
- The paper finds "over 5000 such groups" in the 1000-neuron network. Groups "did not exist at the beginning" and are created by STDP. Because STDP is always on, groups "constantly appear and disappear," and their number "fluctuates between 5000 and 6000." A core of 471 groups survived the whole 24 h simulation. An average group has about 25 neurons, and an average neuron belongs to about 131 groups — [PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/spnet.pdf)
- Activation statistics for the 471 persistent groups: mean activation is about 7 per hour, i.e. some group activates spontaneously roughly every second. Only 917 spikes per neuron per hour, less than 4% of up to 25,000, are part of group activations: "the majority of the spikes are noise." Time-reversed (surrogate) spike trains give far fewer activations, which the paper takes as evidence of significance — [PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/spnet.pdf)
- The website gives different numbers for the released code. The MATLAB run saved at 18000 s (18000.mat) finds 1973 groups (211 with shuffled synapses), or 6071 with the looser setting min_group_path=6. The page notes that "MATLAB and C programs are similar but not equivalent. MATLAB program misses some of the groups" — [abstract page](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/spnet.htm)
- Toy capacity argument (Fig. 10): 5 neurons with delays chosen by hand support 14 polychronous groups, and "adding a sixth neuron triples the number of groups so that there are more groups than synapses." The paper concludes: "it would not be surprising that a network of 10^11 neurons... would have more groups than the number of particles in the universe" — [PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/spnet.pdf)
- Self-identified open problems include: incomplete activation, where precision fades along a group so "the connectivity in the tail of the group does not stabilize"; stability, where spontaneous activity causes "a slow degradation of the memory, and it is not clear how to prevent this"; training on many stimuli; "epileptic seizures" in the regime with more groups than synapses; activity-dependent conduction velocity; and how group counts scale (10^3 neurons gave 10^4 groups) — [PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/spnet.pdf)
- The claim of robustness: "Changing some of the parameters of the model twofold changes the number of groups... but does not eliminate them completely. The self-organization of neurons into polychronous groups is a robust phenomenon." The paper hypothesizes that "unless the brain has an unknown mechanism that specifically prevents polychronization, the real neurons in the mammalian cortex must also self-organize into such groups" — [PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/spnet.pdf)

**Relation to synfire chains and other paradigms (from the paper)**
- On synfire chains, the paper calls them "probably the most beautiful theoretical idea in neuroscience." It says they describe "pools of neurons firing synchronously, not polychronously" and depend on equal or zero delays. Bienenstock (1995) "referred to polychronous activity as a synfire braid." Where synfire research asks whether a chain is stable, Izhikevich uses Edelman's "population thinking": the system is robust "in terms of populations of groups," not individual groups — [PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/spnet.pdf)
- Versus Hopfield-Grossberg attractors: groups "are not attractors." Their activity is transient, lasting 3-4 gamma cycles (<100 ms). Versus feedforward nets: the network is "reentrant" (Edelman) and spontaneously active, so the same stimulus can activate different subsets of groups ("highly degenerate regime"). Versus liquid-state machines: the response depends on the network's history as well as the input — [PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/spnet.pdf)
- Speculation: attention emerges from "regenerative activation" of the groups that represent a stimulus, while groups for unattended stimuli are not inhibited but fire with different timing. When no stimulus is present, spontaneous group activation is likened to "consciousness as attention to memories" — [PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/spnet.pdf)
- The paper credits the idea that STDP selects delay-matched groups to M. Abeles (personal communication), Bienenstock (1995) and Gerstner, Kempter, van Hemmen and Wagner (1996) — [PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/spnet.pdf)

**Follow-up work and critiques**
- Pauli, Weidel, Kunkel and Morrison (2018), "Reproducing polychronization: a guide to maximizing the reproducibility of spiking network models," *Frontiers in Neuroinformatics* 12:46. They re-implemented the network in NEST at three levels: bitwise identical, qualitatively equivalent and generalized. A first NEST attempt found only 5 groups across 10 seeds. Matching the original required nearest-neighbour STDP pairing, a 1 ms shift in spike processing, a particular ordering of LTP and LTD, and replacing an exponential with multiplication by 0.95 — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fninf.2018.00046/full)
- Pauli et al., quantitative findings: the original code reported 4,305 groups, while bitwise reproductions averaged about 2,500 (high-gamma runs) or 1,600 (low-gamma runs). Across 100 seeds, 87% of runs settled in a ~60 Hz state and 13% in a previously unreported ~40 Hz state. All-to-all STDP gave at most 11 groups. Poisson input cut groups by about 90%. A 5 ms maximum delay gave about 35,000 groups with ~27 Hz synchrony. The 1 ms Euler step was numerically unstable (membrane potentials of about 1000 mV), and a 0.1 ms step cut groups from 4,300 to 151 and moved the spectral peak from about 40 to about 25 Hz. They found three bugs in the original group finder; fixing them roughly doubled the groups found. The minimum layer count matters greatly: 5 layers gave 27,116 groups and 10 layers gave 608 — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fninf.2018.00046/full)
- Pauli et al., conclusions: the original study "significantly overstates the number of groups to be found in such networks," mainly through artificial synchrony from the 1 ms resolution. They also judge the null hypothesis for group detection "poorly motivated" — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fninf.2018.00046/full) (as summarized by WebFetch; I read the first ~100k characters)
- Guise, Knott and Benuskova (Otago), 2013 technical reports and ICONIP/AI conference papers on polychronous neural groups (PNGs). Search snippets report that PNG numbers rise during maturation and then plateau, and that shuffling weights sharply reduces them while some survive. The authors read the survivors as groups formed by chance weight configurations. They also frame stability, selectivity, consistency and capacity as requirements for PNGs to serve as representations — [OUCS-2013-02](https://www.cs.otago.ac.nz/research/publications/OUCS-2013-02.pdf); [dblp ausai GuiseKB13](https://dblp1.uni-trier.de/rec/conf/ausai/GuiseKB13.html) (snippet-level only)
- Guise, Knott and Benuskova (2015), "Enhanced polychronization in a spiking network with metaplasticity," *Front. Comput. Neurosci.* 9:9. They used Izhikevich's reference implementation, ran 20 networks of 1000 neurons, and found mean PNG size of 493 with metaplasticity versus 426 without (p<0.001, about 16% larger). They detect groups with a Bayesian "response fingerprinting" method. The paper notes that polychronization is hard to confirm in biological data even though detection in simulation is straightforward — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fncom.2015.00009/full)
- Martinez and Paugam-Moisy (ICANN 2009, LNCS 5769:75-84) distinguish "structural" polychronous groups, which the topology supports, from "dynamical" groups, which are actually activated and form a subset of the structural ones. They give two structural algorithms (one based on delay distributions, one on weights) and one algorithm for activity in a time window — [HAL](https://hal-univ-tlse3.archives-ouvertes.fr/INRIA/inria-00425514)
- Maier and Miller (2008, arXiv:0806.1070) propose a minimal model, independent of neuron model, for studying how the number of polychronous groups depends on connectivity in 1D and 2D architectures — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/0806.1070)
- Szatmary and Izhikevich (2010), *PLoS Comput Biol* 6(8):e1000879, "Spike-timing theory of working memory." In a 1000-neuron network with 1-20 ms delays, about 7,800 polychronous groups emerged (7,825 in Fig. 3). Associative short-term plasticity (time constant about 5 s) lets a cued group reactivate spontaneously (~4 Hz) for tens of seconds, with no persistent-activity attractor. Two groups sharing 66 neurons could be held together, and performance degrades with load — [PLoS CB](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371/journal.pcbi.1000879)
- Eguchi, Isbister, Ahmad and Stringer (2018), *Psychological Review* 125:545-571: a hierarchical spiking model of the ventral visual stream in which polychronization emerges. STDP selects among multiple synaptic contacts with different delays, and "binding neurons" emerge that represent relationships between low- and high-level features ("holographic principle") — [Oxford listing](https://www.psy.ox.ac.uk/publications/857618); [Isbister et al. 2018 Interface Focus, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6015810)
- Izhikevich and Hoppensteadt (2009), "Polychronous wavefront computations," *IJBC* 19:1733-1739: a non-neural computing framework in which pulses spread as circular waves through a medium to "transponders" that fire on coincident inputs. It demonstrates temporal pattern recognition, reverberating memory and logic operations, and is proposed for "nanoscale computational devices" — [PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/pwc.pdf)
- Citation impact (Semantic Scholar, queried 2026-11-24): "Polychronization" has 1,175 citations, 166 of them "influential" — [Semantic Scholar API](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1162/089976606775093882?fields=title,year,citationCount,influentialCitationCount)

### Inferences
- The capacity claim (groups ≫ neurons) counts anatomical subgraphs that could support a cascade. It is not a measure of retrievable memories. Izhikevich's own figures show that most groups are transient, that only 471 of more than 5000 persisted for 24 h, and that activation is partial and fades along the chain. Pauli et al. show the count also depends heavily on numerics and on detection thresholds. "Memory capacity" in an information-theoretic sense (storage plus reliable recall) was never established.
- Group counts disagree across sources: >5000 and 5000-6000 in the paper, 4,305 from the C++ code according to Pauli et al., and 1973 or 6071 for the MATLAB example on the website. This fits Pauli et al.'s point that the number is a function of implementation and analysis parameters.
- Conceptually, polychrony generalizes synfire chains (synchronous pools with equal delays) to heterogeneous delays, i.e. Bienenstock's "synfire braids." The added ingredients are STDP-driven delay selection and the population view taken from Edelman.

### Gaps
- I could not read the full Guise, Knott and Benuskova 2013 reports or their ICONIP paper, so their exact critique of chance-level groups and group stability is known only from snippets.
- I found no peer-reviewed paper that analytically rebuts the "more groups than neurons" capacity claim. The strongest critique found is Pauli et al.'s reproducibility study.
- I found no study that directly confirms delay-based polychronous groups in vivo at millisecond resolution. Search results described the experimental evidence for synfire chains and polychronous groups as "inconclusive," but I could not pin that statement to a specific fetched paper.
- I did not get full citation counts for the 2007 Cerebral Cortex paper (the Semantic Scholar API returned a rate-limit error).

## Q2. "Spike-timing dynamics of neuronal groups" (Izhikevich, Gally & Edelman, Cerebral Cortex 2004)

### Takeaway
This paper came before the 2006 minimal model. It used a 100,000-neuron, 8.5-million-synapse model laid out on a sphere, with distance-dependent conduction delays, STDP and short-term plasticity. In that model, STDP together with delays spontaneously produced "neuronal groups" of time-locked but asynchronous neurons, found with an anchor-neuron algorithm. The paper describes these groups' size, birth rate, lifespan, robustness to synaptic turnover and response to patterned input. It was presented as a spiking-level demonstration of Edelman's theory of neuronal group selection (TNGS).

### Cited Findings
- Citation: Izhikevich E.M., Gally J.A., Edelman G.M. (2004), *Cerebral Cortex* 14:933-944, doi:10.1093/cercor/bhh053. Abstract: 100,000 "reentrantly interconnected neurons" with cortical firing types, receptor kinetics, short-term plasticity, long-term STDP and a distribution of axonal delays. The interplay of STDP and delays "gave rise to the spontaneous formation of neuronal groups — sets of strongly connected neurons capable of firing time-locked, although not necessarily synchronous, spikes" — [PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/reentry.pdf)
- Anatomy: 80,000 excitatory and 20,000 inhibitory neurons are placed at random on a sphere of radius 8 mm, with 8,000,000 excitatory and 500,000 inhibitory synapses. Each excitatory neuron makes 75 local targets within 1.5 mm, plus 25 distant targets via a 12 mm myelinated axon. Conduction velocity is 1 m/s myelinated (after Swadlow) and 0.15 m/s for unmyelinated collaterals, giving local delays of up to 10 ms. Neurons use the 2003 simple model, with heterogeneous parameters spanning RS to CH excitatory types and FS/LTS inhibitory types. Receptors are AMPA, NMDA, GABA_A and GABA_B — [PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/reentry.pdf)
- Group detection used "anchor neurons" (Fig. 4). The 2006 paper later notes that this definition "could not have more groups than neurons (that is, most of the groups in that study went undetected)" — [2004 PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/reentry.pdf); [2006 PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/spnet.pdf)
- Results: most groups had fewer than 100 neurons and spanned 25 ms or more. Groups appeared within about 15 min of model time and then held a dynamic equilibrium of about 900 groups. Shuffling all weights at 1000 min cut this from about 900 to 80, and those 80 disappeared within a minute, so most groups were "activity dependent and needed STDP to appear and persist." Over a 24 h model-time run ("more than a month in real time"), many groups lived less than a few minutes, but "almost 25%" persisted for the whole run. A 100-neuron group had a synaptic connection perturbed about once every 15 min, "providing sufficient time for activity-dependent self-repair" — [PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/reentry.pdf)
- Patterned input: 2 Hz superthreshold stimulation of a 0.5 mm circle increased the groups originating there 60-fold in 5 model minutes and doubled the total group count, while mean firing stayed below 10 Hz. Synchronous stimulation of two circles enlarged one projective field 100-fold (cooperation). Asynchronous stimulation showed competition, with shared neurons only 10% of the synchronous case. The paper likens these shifts to Merzenich-style receptive-field remapping — [PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/reentry.pdf)
- The paper frames itself against Pearson et al. (1987), an earlier TNGS simulation that "did not, however, explore the detailed dynamics of groups of spiking neurons with millisecond spike-timing precision" — [PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/reentry.pdf)
- Citation impact: 539 citations, 31 "influential" (Semantic Scholar, queried 2026-11-24) — [Semantic Scholar API](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1093/cercor/bhh053?fields=title,year,citationCount,influentialCitationCount)

### Inferences
- The 2004 paper is the empirical origin of polychronization, although it used "neuronal group" instead of "polychronous group." The 2006 paper mainly (a) shrank the model to 1000 neurons, (b) changed the group definition from anchor-based to triplet-based, which made counts above N possible, and (c) added the capacity, attention and consciousness speculations.
- The phrase "reentrantly interconnected" and the measurements of group birth, death and competition apply TNGS concepts (selection among groups, reentry, degeneracy) directly at spike-timing resolution.

### Gaps
- I did not extract the exact group counts per condition from the figures (e.g., Fig. 10's time series), only the numbers given in the text.
- I found no published critique aimed specifically at the 2004 paper. Later critiques (e.g., Pauli et al.) target the 2006 minimal model.

## Q3. "Solving the distal reward problem through linkage of STDP and dopamine signaling" (Cerebral Cortex 2007) and its influence on three-factor learning rules

### Takeaway
Izhikevich proposed that each synapse carries a slowly decaying eligibility trace c (τ_c = 1 s), set by STDP, and that the weight changes only as ṡ = c·d, where d is extracellular dopamine. Rare, precisely timed coincidences can then be credited by rewards arriving 1-3 s later, even while the network is continuously active. This is the property he said distinguished his model from earlier eligibility-trace models, which needed the network to be quiet between action and reward. The model reproduced instrumental and Pavlovian conditioning and the shift of the dopamine response from US to CS, but it had no dopamine dip and no true TD error. The paper became one of the canonical references for "reward-modulated STDP" (R-STDP) and neo-Hebbian three-factor rules. Later theory (Legenstein, Pecevski and Maass 2008; Frémaux, Sprekeler and Gerstner 2010) showed that such rules need the third factor to be reward minus expected reward, i.e. a critic, to avoid an unsupervised bias. From 2014 onward, experiments (Yagishita et al. 2014 and others) found eligibility windows of about 1 s, close to the time scale Izhikevich and others had assumed.

### Cited Findings
**The model**
- Citation: Izhikevich E.M. (2007), *Cerebral Cortex* 17:2443-2452, doi:10.1093/cercor/bhl152. The abstract poses the conundrum: how does the brain know which firing patterns caused a reward "if (1) the patterns are no longer there when the reward arrives and (2) most neurons and synapses are active during the waiting period." It continues: "Random firings during the waiting period to the reward do not affect STDP... the key feature that distinguishes our approach from previous theoretical studies, which implicitly assume that the network be quiet during the waiting period or that the patterns be preserved until the reward arrives" — [abstract page](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/dastdp.htm)
- Equations: ċ = −c/τ_c + STDP(τ)·δ(t − t_pre/post), with τ_c = 1 s, so that the effect of dopamine is "negligible 5 s after the STDP-triggered event"; ṡ = c·d; ḋ = −d/τ_d + DA(t), with τ_d ≈ 0.2 s. Tonic DA input is 0.01 µM/s, giving a baseline of about 2 nM. c is explicitly called the "eligibility trace" or "synaptic tag" (citing Klopf 1982, Sutton and Barto 1998, Houk et al. 1995). The LTD area of the STDP curve is 50% larger than the LTP area, and only excitatory synapses are plastic — [PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/dastdp.pdf)
- The paper says its key assumption, that "DA has a permissive, enabling effect allowing STDP to take place", is "a testable assumption that has never been suggested before," and calls it a "postdiction" grounded in DA modulation of classical LTP/LTD — [PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/dastdp.pdf)

**Results**
- Network: 1000 neurons (800 RS excitatory, 200 FS inhibitory), about 100,000 synapses, 10% connectivity, maximum delay 1 ms, firing around 1 Hz — [PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/dastdp.pdf)
- Single-synapse instrumental conditioning (Fig. 1): reward arrives 1-3 s after each event in which a chosen postsynaptic neuron fires within 10 ms of its presynaptic partner. The chosen synapse (initially s = 0) is potentiated to maximum while the other 79,999 excitatory synapses are not. In 50 simulated experiments it reached maximum within 1 h, needing "only 40 ± 8" rewards — [PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/dastdp.pdf)
- Pavlovian conditioning (Fig. 2): 100 random stimuli S1-S100 of 50 neurons each arrive at about 5 per second. S1 is rewarded with a delay of up to 1 s. Within about 100 pairings (the first hour) the network's response to S1 is enhanced and the outgoing synapses from S1 strengthen — [PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/dastdp.pdf)
- Instrumental stimulus-response (Fig. 3): a stimulus group S and response groups A and B, 50 neurons each. Rewarding response A (|A|>|B| within 20 ms) raised P(A) to about 80% in under 100 trials. Switching the reward to B reversed the behavior in under 50 trials, and learning succeeded in 20 of 20 repetitions. The network "does not know the identity of neurons in A and B, or what is rewarded" — [PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/dastdp.pdf)
- DA-response shift (Fig. 4): with US, CS1, CS2 and "VTAp" (VTA-projecting) groups of 100 neurons and US→VTAp synapses preset to maximum, the VTAp response shifts from US to CS1 and then to the earlier CS2. Calling this "the first step toward a spiking network implementation of the TD error," the paper notes that the response jumps rather than shifting gradually in latency, "more consistent with... Pan et al. 2005" — [PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/dastdp.pdf)
- Acknowledged limitations: the DA response "is not a true error prediction signal required by TD algorithms because the model fails to exhibit depression of firing rate (dip)... when US is omitted," since there is "no internal clock." An unexpected US after training gives a diminished response, unlike in vivo recordings. Full TD would need basal ganglia loops — [PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/dastdp.pdf)
- The paper argues the mechanism works only for precise spike-timing patterns. Rate-based learning would fail, because a cue "does not increase the firing rate of any neuron; it just adds, removes, or changes the time of a single spike." Conversely, DA-STDP would fail to reinforce rate patterns, since STDP dominated by LTD depresses synapses under rate fluctuations. Izhikevich credits Gerstner with this point, and credits Olaf Sporns (2000) with pointing out the credit-assignment problem to him. Punishment can be modelled as d below baseline, or as DA gating only LTP. The paper predicts that the STDP curve "will look quite different if DA is present during or immediately after the induction" — [PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/dastdp.pdf)
- Earlier spiking reinforcement-learning proposals that Izhikevich criticizes for requiring a quiet waiting period: Seung (2003), Hasselmo (2005), Koene and Hasselmo (2005) and eligibility-trace models such as Houk et al. (1995) — [PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/dastdp.pdf)

**Influence and later critiques**
- Frémaux and Gerstner (2016), "Neuromodulated spike-timing-dependent plasticity, and theory of three-factor learning rules," *Front. Neural Circuits* 9:85, list R-STDP models: Farries and Fairhall (2007), Florian (2007), Izhikevich (2007), Legenstein et al. (2008), Vasilaki et al. (2009), Frémaux et al. (2010) and Friedrich et al. (2011). They class Izhikevich's scheme as "gated-Hebbian" learning, in which "sparse, positive rewards are given to reinforce the occurrence of pre-before-post spiking events at particular synapses." His STDP window and activity are balanced so that the mean Hebbian term is slightly negative, which "causes a downward drift of all synaptic weights" except the rewarded one. They describe eligibility-trace reinforcement of recent pairings as a heuristic "with a weaker theoretical foundation" (citing Klopf, Seung, Farries and Fairhall, Florian, Izhikevich), and note that covariance-type rules "typically need thousands of trials to converge" — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fncir.2015.00085/full)
- Frémaux, Sprekeler and Gerstner (2010), *J. Neurosci.* 30(40):13326-13337, "Functional requirements for reward-modulated STDP." Most R-STDP rules mix a reward-sensitive term with an "unsupervised" reward-independent term that generally hurts learning. That term can be suppressed only if the neuromodulator encodes reward minus expected reward, with the expected reward computed correctly and specific to the task (a critic) — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6634722); [Frontiers review](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fncir.2015.00085/full)
- Legenstein, Pecevski and Maass (2008), "A learning theory for reward-modulated STDP with application to biofeedback," *PLoS Comput Biol*, plus a NIPS 2007 companion paper. They give analytic conditions under which R-STDP achieves a learning goal, reproduce the Fetz and Baker monkey biofeedback result, show learning of temporal (not just spatial) pattern classification, and treat spontaneous variability as exploration. The ModelDB entry for their model cites Izhikevich (2007) — [TU Graz abstract](https://igi-web.tugraz.at/people/Abstracts/LegensteinETAL:08a); [ModelDB](https://modeldb.science/116837?tab=7)
- Gerstner, Lehmann, Liakoni, Corneil and Brea (2018), "Eligibility traces and plasticity on behavioral time scales: experimental support of neoHebbian three-factor learning rules" (*Front. Neural Circuits*; arXiv:1801.05219). They write the general rule as ẇ = e_ij·M^3rd(t), citing "Izhikevich, 2007; Legenstein et al., 2008; Frémaux et al., 2013." They say theory predicted eligibility traces of "about 200ms to 2s," quoting Izhikevich's "τ_c = 1s" as an example. They argue that experimental evidence for eligibility traces "on the time scale of seconds has been collected only during the last few years." They review Yagishita et al. 2014 (striatum), He et al. 2015 (cortex; norepinephrine and serotonin), Brzosko et al. 2015 and 2017 (hippocampus; dopamine) and Bittner et al. 2017. On theory they write: "Assumptions are predictions" — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1801.05219)
- Yagishita et al. (2014), *Science* 345:1616: in nucleus accumbens spines, dopamine (optogenetic) promoted spine enlargement only within about 0.3-2 s of a 1 s glutamate-uncaging plus spike STDP protocol, an effect dependent on DARPP-32/PP-1. In Gerstner et al.'s summary, the maximum effect came with dopamine during the protocol (d = −0.4 s) and LTP was still visible at d = 1 s, but there was no effect at d = −2 s or d = +4 s, i.e. an eligibility decay "on a time scale of 1s" — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4225776); [Gerstner et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1801.05219)
- Frémaux and Gerstner (2016) note that synaptic "tags" in the experimental literature (Frey and Morris) decay over about an hour, much longer than theoretical eligibility traces of hundreds of ms to seconds, and that "the timing requirement between spike pairings and dopamine deserves additional experimental investigation" — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fncir.2015.00085/full)

### Inferences
- The 2007 paper's lasting contribution is conceptual and computational rather than new mathematics. It gave a minimal, simulation-ready formulation (c-trace plus DA gate), released as MATLAB/C code, which became a default R-STDP template. Theory papers from 2008-2010 then formalized when such rules work.
- The main theoretical weakness is that raw reward (not a reward prediction error) gates plasticity. Izhikevich offset the resulting unsupervised drift with an LTD-dominated STDP window and sparse rewards, which Frémaux and Gerstner describe as "gated-Hebbian." This works for single-target credit assignment but does not extend to multiple tasks without a critic.
- The τ_c ≈ 1 s eligibility trace looks prescient in light of Yagishita et al. (2014). Gerstner et al. explicitly present the seconds-scale trace as a theoretical prediction later supported by experiment.

### Gaps
- I did not fetch Florian (2007) or Farries and Fairhall (2007) directly, so whether their work was independent of or simultaneous with Izhikevich's is known only from the review lists.
- I could not obtain current citation counts for the 2007 paper (rate-limited).
- Neuromorphic and robotics uses of DA-STDP (e.g., at Brain Corp) were not researched in depth.

## Q4. "Large-scale model of mammalian thalamocortical systems" (Izhikevich & Edelman, PNAS 2008) and the 2005 10^11-neuron simulation

### Takeaway
The 2008 PNAS model simulated 1 million multicompartment spiking neurons (22 types) and almost 0.5 billion synapses. It placed them on a human cortical surface derived from DTI (part of the data came from Izhikevich's own brain), with cat-V1 laminar microcircuitry, thalamic nuclei, short-term plasticity and dendritic STDP modulated by dopamine. Emergent results included self-sustained activity after STDP tuning, delta, alpha, beta and gamma rhythms that differed by region, propagating waves, anticorrelated fronto-parietal fMRI-like oscillations, and extreme sensitivity to a single extra spike (whole-cortex divergence in under 0.5 s). Separately, on 27 October 2005 Izhikevich finished a one-second run of a 10^11-neuron, ~10^15-synapse thalamocortical model that took about 50 days on 27 processors. He said himself that this run made no scientific contribution beyond learning what such scale requires.

### Cited Findings
**PNAS 2008**
- Citation: Izhikevich E.M. and Edelman G.M. (2008), *PNAS* 105(9):3593-3598 (March 4, 2008). The model spans three scales: (i) global white-matter anatomy "obtained by means of diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) of a human brain"; (ii) thalamic nuclei and 6-layer cortical microcircuitry from "in vitro labeling and three-dimensional reconstruction of single neurons of cat visual cortex"; (iii) "22 basic types of neurons" with laminar dendritic trees. "One million multicompartmental spiking neurons calibrated to reproduce known types of responses recorded in vitro in rats... almost half a billion synapses with appropriate receptor kinetics, short-term plasticity, and long-term dendritic spike-timing-dependent synaptic plasticity (dendritic STDP)" — [abstract page](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/large-scale_model_of_human_brain.htm)
- Data sources: microcircuitry from Binzegger, Douglas and Martin (cat area 17). DTI analysed with the "TensorLine" algorithm. Two DTI datasets were used, "one of the brain of the first author (E.M.I.)" and one from Gordon Kindlmann / Andrew Alexander. Experimental data come from three species (human, cat, rat). The paper admits that "small bundles of fibers in the human brain were inevitably missed because DTI does not presently have sufficient resolution." Axonal delays were up to 20 ms. Dendritic STDP "develops slowly with time with a rate modulated by dopamine" — [PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/large-scale_model_of_human_brain.pdf)
- Computing: C with MPI on a Beowulf cluster of 60 3 GHz processors with 1.5 GB RAM each. Initialization takes about 10 min, and "one minute to compute one second of simulated data using a sub-millisecond time step" — [PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/large-scale_model_of_human_brain.pdf)
- Spontaneous activity: started from seed spikes, activity "died out during the first second" regardless of network size ("we tested up to 10 million neurons"). The authors added "minis" (1 spontaneous release per synapse per second) for 30 min while STDP ran. Citing their polychronization work, they found that after the minis were turned off the network stayed active, because "STDP had fine-tuned the synaptic connectivity" for reverberating polychronous activity. This "required >10,000 neurons" — [PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/large-scale_model_of_human_brain.pdf)
- Single-spike sensitivity: adding or removing one spike of one neuron "completely changed the state of the entire cortex in <0.5 s." Divergence took about 400 ms on average, with no dependence on the location or type of the perturbed neuron, and became faster as network size grew. The authors are explicitly ambivalent. On one reading, "only firing patterns in a statistical sense should be considered"; on the other, "every spike of every neuron counts." They cite experiments in which microstimulation of a single tactile afferent, or of single rat cortical neurons, affects perception or behavior — [PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/large-scale_model_of_human_brain.pdf)
- Rhythms and waves: local gamma (40-50 Hz) with phases that varied across locations, and so was weak in the global spectrum, consistent with gamma being weaker in EEG/MEG. Dominant delta (1-3 Hz) and alpha (~10 Hz) rhythms, plus strong beta (~20 Hz) in some regions, even though the microcircuitry was identical everywhere. Propagating waves appeared and disappeared spontaneously. The simulated fMRI signal showed slow fronto-parietal anti-phase oscillations "as seen in humans" — [PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/large-scale_model_of_human_brain.pdf)
- Proposed future uses: simulating lesions, strokes and tumours; DTI of patients with Alzheimer's or Parkinson's; drug effects; cholinergic sleep modulation; and "eventually test various hypotheses on how discriminatory perception and consciousness arise." Funding came from the Neurosciences Research Foundation and NSF grant CCF-0523156 — [PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/large-scale_model_of_human_brain.pdf)
- Citation impact: 1,107 citations, 43 "influential" (Semantic Scholar, queried 2026-11-24) — [Semantic Scholar API](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1073/pnas.0712231105?fields=title,year,citationCount,influentialCitationCount)

**2005 human-scale simulation**
- "On October 27, 2005 I finished simulation of a model that has the size of the human brain. The model has 100,000,000,000 neurons... and almost 1,000,000,000,000,000 (one quadrillion or 10^15) synapses. It represents 300x300 mm^2 of mammalian thalamo-cortical surface, specific, non-specific, and reticular thalamic nuclei... The model exhibited alpha and gamma rhythms, moving clusters of neurons in up- and down-states... One second of simulation took 50 days on a beowulf cluster of 27 processors (3GHz each)" — [izhikevich.org](https://www.izhikevich.org/human_brain_simulation/Blue_Brain.htm)
- "Why did I do that?": "no significant contribution to neuroscience could be made by simulating one second of a model, even if it has the size of the human brain. However, I learned what it takes." Because 2^32 < 10^11, standard integers cannot index the neurons, and storing the weights would need "10,000 terabytes." The workaround: "Instead of saving synaptic connections, I regenerated the anatomy every time step (1 ms)" — [izhikevich.org/why](https://www.izhikevich.org/human_brain_simulation/why.htm)
- The same page extrapolates with Moore's law from the benchmark "1 sec = 50 days on 27 3GHz processors," projecting real-time human-brain simulation on a single processor by July 2046. It also says "many essential details of the anatomy and dynamics of the mammalian nervous system would probably be still unknown." Take-home message: "Size doesn't matter; it's what you put into your model and how you embed it into the environment (to close the loop)" — [izhikevich.org/why](https://www.izhikevich.org/human_brain_simulation/why.htm)
- The polychronization paper (in press 2005) mentions the same run: 10^11 neurons, "6-layer cortical microcircuitry, specific, non-specific, and reticular thalamic nuclei. One second of simulation took more than one month on a cluster of 27 3-GHz processors" — [PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/spnet.pdf)

**Related experimental test of single-spike sensitivity**
- London, Roth, Beeren, Häusser and Latham (2010), *Nature* 466:123-127: in vivo in rat barrel cortex, one extra spike produced about 28 extra spikes in postsynaptic targets. They infer that this amplification creates stimulus-independent noise of about 2.2-4.5 mV, that synfire-like precise events were rare, and that cortex probably relies mainly on a rate code — [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature09086)

### Inferences
- The model's single-spike chaos (divergence in under 0.5 s) cuts against the precise-timing programme of polychronization. Izhikevich and Edelman left the interpretation open. London et al. (2010) later read in vivo perturbation sensitivity as evidence for noise and rate coding. That is a substantive counter-reading, though I did not verify whether London et al. cite Izhikevich and Edelman.
- The PNAS model links Izhikevich's lines of work: the simple neuron model (extended to compartments), polychronous self-organization (needed to sustain activity), DA-modulated STDP, and Edelman's reentrant thalamocortical "dynamic core" programme.
- The 10^11 run was a feasibility and engineering stunt: one second of model time, anatomy regenerated rather than stored, no plasticity or learning reported. Izhikevich describes it that way himself. Popular accounts that present it as a "human brain simulation" overstate its meaning.
- The "22 basic types" in the PNAS abstract and the "21 basic types" on the project web page conflict slightly, probably a counting difference between cortical and thalamic types.

### Gaps
- I found no published peer critique aimed specifically at the PNAS 2008 model (e.g., on validating its rhythms or its DTI-derived connectivity). Search found only secondary blog commentary and citations in later thalamocortical models.
- The 2005 10^11 simulation was never published in a peer-reviewed paper as far as I could find. Its documentation is the web page, the polychronization paper's footnote and movies.
- I could not confirm whether the PNAS model's dopamine modulation was exercised in the reported results or only implemented.

## Q5. Other works in this period: STDP-BCM (2003), Scholarpedia, consciousness, wavefront computing, working memory and others

### Takeaway
Izhikevich and Desai (2003) showed analytically that nearest-neighbour STDP applied to Poisson spike trains reduces to the BCM rule, with a potentiation threshold of about 12 Hz that slides with small changes in STDP parameters. This later became a standard link in plasticity theory (triplet STDP, Clopath). Other outputs in the period include founding and editing Scholarpedia (2006 onward) and writing articles there (Bursting, FitzHugh-Nagumo, Equilibrium, Phase model, State space, Game of Life), a 2006 PNAS paper on consciousness measures with Seth, Reeke and Edelman, polychronous wavefront computing (2009), the spike-timing theory of working memory (2010) and "Hybrid spiking models" (2010). "Gamma oscillation by synaptic inhibition..." does not appear in his publication list.

### Cited Findings
- Izhikevich and Desai (2003), "Relating STDP to BCM," *Neural Computation* 15:1511-1523 (communicated by T. Sejnowski): "the BCM learning rule follows directly from STDP when pre- and postsynaptic neurons fire uncorrelated or weakly correlated Poisson spike trains, and only nearest-neighbor spike interactions are taken into account." They compare all-to-all, semi-nearest-neighbour and nearest-neighbour implementations, both additive and multiplicative. Using Froemke and Dan's (2002) parameters, they calculate "a threshold value of around 12 Hz." Changing the STDP time constant by "as little as 10%" slides the threshold "by a factor of two," which accounts for the BCM sliding threshold — [PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/bcm.pdf)
- Gerstner et al. (2018) group Izhikevich and Desai (2003) with Senn et al. (2001) and Froemke and Dan (2002) as nonlinear STDP models closely related to triplet STDP (Pfister et al. 2006), the Clopath model and BCM — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1801.05219)
- Citation impact: "Relating STDP to BCM" has 347 citations, 38 "influential" (Semantic Scholar, queried 2026-11-24) — [Semantic Scholar API](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1162/089976603321891783?fields=title,year,citationCount,influentialCitationCount)
- Izhikevich, Desai, Walcott and Hoppensteadt (2003), "Bursts as a unit of neural information: selective communication via resonance," *Trends in Neurosciences* 26:161-167 — [publication list](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/index.htm)
- Publication list items from the period (the page is headed "old (NSI) list of publications"): "Bursting" Scholarpedia 1(3):1300 (2006); "FitzHugh-Nagumo Model" with R. FitzHugh, Scholarpedia 1(9):1349 (2006); "Weakly Coupled Oscillators" with Y. Kuramoto, Encyclopedia of Mathematical Physics (2006); "Equilibrium" Scholarpedia 2(10):2014 (2007); "Phase model" with G.B. Ermentrout, Scholarpedia 3(10):1487 (2008); "State space" with D. Terman, Scholarpedia 3(3):1924 (2008); Seth, Izhikevich, Reeke and Edelman (2006), "Theories and measures of consciousness: an extended framework," PNAS; Izhikevich and Hoppensteadt (2004), "Classification of bursting mappings," IJBC; Wu et al. (2005), persistent sodium currents in Mesencephalic V neurons, J Neurophysiol; "Hybrid spiking models," Phil. Trans. R. Soc. A 368:5061-5070 (2010); and later "Game of Life" with J.H. Conway and A. - Izhikevich is listed as Editor-in-Chief of Scholarpedia and as curator of "Bursting" (with NSI affiliation). DBLP credits him with "Bursting" (1300) and "FitzHugh-Nagumo model" (1349, with FitzHugh) in Scholarpedia volume 1 — [publication list](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/index.htm); [dblp](https://dblp.org/db/journals/scholarpedia/scholarpedia1)
- Polychronous wavefront computations (2009) and the spike-timing theory of working memory (2010): see Q1 — [pwc PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/pwc.pdf); [PLoS CB](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371/journal.pcbi.1000879)

### Inferences
- The STDP-to-BCM result fits a recurring pattern in Izhikevich's work: showing that phenomenological rules (BCM, LTP/LTD) or neuron classes emerge from simpler spike-level mechanisms. It also supplied the nearest-neighbour STDP convention that his later network models depend on. Pauli et al. (2018) found that nearest-neighbour pairing, as opposed to all-to-all, was essential for polychronous group formation in the 2006 model.

- I did not read the 2006 consciousness PNAS paper (Seth et al.) or "Hybrid spiking models" (2010) in detail.

## Q6. Relationship to Edelman's neural Darwinism / The Neurosciences Institute, and comparison with Blue Brain and other large-scale efforts

### Takeaway
Izhikevich's network work from 2000 to about 2009 was done at Edelman's Neurosciences Institute, and it is explicitly a spiking-level elaboration of the theory of neuronal group selection (TNGS). Its key elements map onto TNGS: neuronal groups selected by experience via STDP, reentrant anatomy, degeneracy, population thinking, value systems via dopamine, and embodiment ("the brain is embodied, the body is embedded"). Izhikevich positioned his large-scale models as broader in scope and less detailed per neuron than the Blue Brain Project, which modelled one cortical column (later a ~31,000-neuron microcircuit) with full biophysical detail. He aimed at whole-brain, anatomically global but phenomenologically reduced models. In 2009 he co-founded Brain Corporation.

### Cited Findings
- TNGS has three components: developmental selection, experiential selection (constrained by value systems) and reentry. Its units are neuronal groups of hundreds to thousands of strongly interconnected neurons. Francis Crick derided it as "neural Edelmanism" — [PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3111444); [Edelman 1993 (brainmaps)](https://brainmaps.org/pdf/edelman1993.pdf)
- The 2004 paper presents itself as TNGS at millisecond resolution, citing "the theory of neuronal group selection (TNGS; Edelman, 1987, 1993)" and "reentrant signaling" — [PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/reentry.pdf)
- The 2006 paper invokes "population thinking (Edelman, 1987)," "reentrant (Edelman, 1987)" anatomy and "highly degenerate regime (Edelman & Gally, 2001)." Its "Closing the loop" passage reads: "The philosophy at the Neurosciences Institute (the author's host institute) is 'the brain is embodied, the body is embedded'," citing Krichmar and Edelman (2002) — [PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/spnet.pdf)
- Author contributions on the PNAS 2008 paper: "E.M.I. and G.M.E. designed research; E.M.I. performed research" — [PDF](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/large-scale_model_of_human_brain.pdf)
- Izhikevich moved to San Diego in 2000 for a fellowship in theoretical neurobiology at NSI, founded Scholarpedia there, and co-founded Brain Corp in 2009 — [Brain Corp profile](https://braincorp.com/team/dr-eugene-izhikevich)
- Izhikevich's own comparison: "This research has a more ambitious goal than the Blue Brain Project conducted by IBM and EPFL... The Blue Brain Project builds a small part of the brain that represents a cortical column, though to a much greater detail than the models I develop. In contrast, my goal is a large-scale biologically accurate computer model of the whole human brain. At present, it has only cortex and thalamus; other subcortical structures, including hippocampus, cerebellum, basal ganglia, etc., will be added later" — [izhikevich.org](https://www.izhikevich.org/human_brain_simulation/Blue_Brain.htm)
- Blue Brain's 2015 milestone: Markram et al., "Reconstruction and simulation of neocortical microcircuitry," *Cell* 163:456-492. About 31,000 neurons in about 0.29 mm³ of juvenile rat somatosensory cortex, about 8 million connections and about 37 million synapses, reproducing in vitro and in vivo experiments without parameter tuning — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26451489/); [summary listing](https://www.ccs.upm.es/research/publications/reconstruction-and-simulation-of-neocortical-microcircuitry/)
- The 2009 IBM "cat-scale" controversy: Modha's team (IBM with LBNL) won the Gordon Bell Prize at SC09 for a cortical simulation of about 1.6 billion neurons and 10 trillion synapses. Markram's open letter called it "a mass deception" and "light years away from a cat brain," objecting to the "simplest possible equation you can imagine to simulate a neuron." IBM replied that it had made "a cortical simulation at the scale that a cat brain functions" — [IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/blue-brain-project-leader-angry-about-cat-brain); [eWeek](https://www.eweek.com/networking/rival-scientist-calls-ibm-cat-brain-simulation-a-scam/)
- A 2022 bioRxiv thalamocortical reimplementation of Blue Brain's rat S1 cites Izhikevich and Edelman (2008) among prior thalamocortical models — [bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.02.03.479029v2.full.pdf)

### Inferences
- There are two contrasting philosophies. Izhikevich: phenomenological neurons (2-variable models with compartments), global anatomy (DTI), emergent dynamics and scale before detail. Markram/Blue Brain: biophysically detailed Hodgkin-Huxley-type neurons, dense local reconstruction, detail before scale. IBM's 2009 simulation was closer to Izhikevich's style (simple neurons at huge scale) and drew Markram's "simplest possible equation" critique. The same critique implicitly applies to Izhikevich's 10^11-neuron run, but I found no source in which Markram names Izhikevich.
- Izhikevich's own take-home message ("Size doesn't matter; it's what you put into your model and how you embed it into the environment") anticipates the critique that scale alone is not insight. It matches NSI's embodied "brain-based devices" programme, and arguably his later move to robotics at Brain Corp.
- Polychronous groups are, in effect, a spike-timing reinterpretation of Edelman's "neuronal groups": selection by STDP from a repertoire of anatomically possible groups, with degeneracy because the same neurons serve many groups. The 2006 capacity and consciousness speculations serve TNGS-style theorizing about consciousness (cf. Seth, Izhikevich, Reeke and Edelman 2006).

### Gaps
- I found no published head-to-head comparison or exchange between Izhikevich and the Blue Brain team.
- I found no direct discussion of how Izhikevich's large-scale models relate to later efforts such as Spaun (Eliasmith 2012), the Human Brain Project or Potjans-Diesmann microcircuit models. Any such comparison would be inference only.
- The exact date of Izhikevich's departure from NSI and its relation to Brain Corp's founding (2009) is not documented beyond Brain Corp's profile.
